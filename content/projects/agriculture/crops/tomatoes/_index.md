## Tomatoes

</br>

### About this crop
Tomatoes are one of the most frequent and valuable crops in small-scale Spanish agriculture. They are very polyvalent and can be used in a great variety of dishes, ranging from salads to broths. The plants are leafy, vine-like and able to grow quite tall, despite the stem not being able to support its weight after a month of growth.

While home-grown tomatoes are much better tasting than the ones found in supermarkets or commercial stores they are more fragile than most other crops covered here, therefore requiring more attention and care. 

### Requirements and planting
As most other plants that produce fruit with much water, tomatoes are better suited for warm to hot climates, for sun greatly improves the speed at which the plant grows and fruit ripens. Providing enough water is positive but it should not be overdone, specially on the early stages of development. Excessive humidity is detrimental to most varieties, as it is tied with diseases listed below. Plants and fruit are extremely sensitive to frost, if your climate has these consider covering the plant during nights or cold days. As a fun fact, rural farmers in southern Spain (mediterranean climate that is much warmer during the day but more prone to frost on spring nights) use all sorts of home-made covers. These can range from large bottles or other propped-up plastics to even old repurposed roof tiles.

Tomatoes are very forgiving with soil conditions. Loose soil facilitates root development but you can get away with planting on more compact variants, such as those rich in clay. If the soil is poor in nutrients, using organic fertilizer, compost or manure is recommended. There should be at least 40-50 cm of separation between tomato plants to avoid cluttering, ensure sunlight is able to reach all parts of the plants and allow for adequate airflow.

I buy pre-grown seedlings instead of plain seeds because they are more common in local stores and require less effort to kickstart. I have never tried to grow tomato plants from a previous tomato fruit's seeds but it is possible, even if seeds are stored over winter. When using seedlings, tomatoes can be planted on April or May depending on temperature and how favorable the upcoming weather forecasts are. If you are using seeds, you will have to start about 4 weeks sooner and perhaps let the seeds grow indoors or in a warmer environment. 

### Care and maintenance
Unlike most other crops, tomatoes do require periodic maintenance to ensure the plant survives and to improve production. After the tomato plant grows, it will become too tall and the weight of the leaves will bend and break the stem. It is required to add some sort of vertical support (a bamboo cane, flexible tree branch, rebar...) that is nailed to the ground like a stake, and tie the main stem of the plant to the support. The support must be as long as the plant and able to withstand its weight and probably some wind. The plant can be tied with strips of old cloth (my prefered choice), twist ties or even thin rope. It is important to leave some slack and ensure that no small branches or tomatoes are caught between the main stem and the support, as they will grow larger, probably strangle the plant and impede growth. 

As is the case with trees, vines and bushes, tomatoes also like to grow ludicrous amounts of leaves and branches. Likewise, it is common practice to perform some pruning. Throughout the plant's life, so called 'suckers' will appear between the main stem and side branches. Unlike the side branches, these sucker branches can be safely pruned, as they add weight and more leaves to the plant and, suposedly, slow down fruit growth. I personally like to remove suckers, but it is a topic open to debate and a lot of farmers choose not to.

Later down the plant's life, it is possible to remove some of the branches that are closer to the ground. This is best done when the plant is close to it's final height and tomatoes are starting to develop. Branches with flowers or tomatoes should never be pruned, unless they show signs of disease. 

### Diseases and pests
While tomatoes can suffer from multiple types of diseases and pests, locally, the only one that is common is <a href="/projects/agriculture/kb/diseases/#mildew">mildew</a>. Standard treatment and prevention techniques apply. Tomato variants selectively bred to withstand to mildew exist and have fared pretty well for me but taste blander in comparison to vulnerable variants. 

### Harvest
Harvest is trivial: tomatoes are ready to pick when they become a bit squishy and reach their final color (generally red, but some variants are yellow or black). When in doubt, try one that looks ripe and take note if it is not.  

Tomatoes are mostly water-based, therefore having a relatively short shelf-life of up to a couple of weeks once ripe. Consuming them fresh is ideal, but they can also be canned whole or made into tomato sauce and canned too. While tomatoes can be frozen, the process will make water expand and tear all the fibers of the fruit, rendering them mushy and unpleasant for raw consumption (though they can still be used for soups, sauces and other recipes that require cooking them).
